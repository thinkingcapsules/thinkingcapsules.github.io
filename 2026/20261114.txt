[Emilio Dal Bo](https://www.trasumanare.it/p/attenzione-nuovo-petrolio), who writes an Italian newsletter on personal growth, opens with a comparison: a sixteenth-century scholar took in about 80 GB of information in a whole life, roughly what an average person takes in today in a single day. Attention has replaced oil as the resource everyone drills for. He borrows an image from the art critic Philippe Daverio: like the fire in Plato's cave, the media light only what is placed in front of them, and whoever decides what reaches the wall controls the story people share. Content that pays quickly beats content that asks for time. So coverage of youth protests showed mostly bins and broken windows, because violence earns more than an analysis of reasons: economics more than bad faith. He ends by asking readers to choose what enters their minds as carefully as what they eat.

The diagnosis holds, and the point about bad faith is the fairest part: nobody has to lie for the picture to be distorted. [What you keep noticing is the real inventory of what you care about](post.html?p=2026/20260405.txt), and much of that inventory is now written by someone else.

The remedy is [a better diet](post.html?p=2026/20261105.txt), and it still leaves you in the cave, choosing among shadows. Plato's prisoner isn't freed by a better selection on the wall. He's freed by turning around toward the light. Jesus describes attention the same way: [your eye is a lamp that provides light for your body](https://thesouldevotional.github.io/soul/post.html?p=bible/matthew_6-19-34.txt#v22), and what it faces fills you. Attention isn't only something to protect. Simone Weil called it the rarest and purest form of generosity. Given to God and to the person in front of you, it is no longer there to be extracted.

!!Attention you give away is attention nobody can take.!!
